The multiplier decision is arithmetic.
It is not a prediction.

Every indexed policy asks the same question: pay for the higher participation rate, or don't. The industry answers it with a guess about next year. There is a better answer, and it doesn't require knowing anything about next year at all.

Read this before the Mortgage Killer or Time Machine pages

Those pages show what a policy did. This one shows which version of the policy you should have owned — and it is the only page here that answers that with math rather than opinion. The numbers on the other two are only as good as the option chosen on this one.

The one insight

A multiplier costs a known, contractual charge. It pays a higher share of whatever the index does. So there is exactly one index return where the two options credit the same amount. Below it the multiplier loses by a knowable amount. Above it, it wins by a knowable amount.

That threshold is computable today, to the basis point, with no forecast of any kind.

And here is what almost everyone misses. In a floored product, the multiplier is worst exactly when the floor matters. A 0% year credits zero either way — but the charge is still taken. The multiplier does not protect you in a bad year. It costs you more in one.

So the decision turns on how many floor years the holding period contains — not on next year's return. And floor frequency over 30 years is vastly more knowable than next year.

The 80%-every-year loan strategy

Pay the full premium. Borrow 80% of surrender value every year. Participating loan, so the borrowed money keeps earning. Tested against real history with real charges.

It lapses.

Every start year tested. Every charge assumption tested. Including a 0% loan rate with no charges at all — literally free money — it still lapses, because drawing 80% of surrender value annually drains the account faster than it can compound, while cost of insurance on the rising net amount at risk accelerates.

A lapse with a loan outstanding is the worst outcome in this platform: the gain becomes ordinary income in that year, with no policy left and no cash to pay the bill.

The sustainable number is 5–10%, not 80%. At a 5% annual draw the policy survives with the loan peaking at 77% of account value and real equity left. At 10% it survives but peaks at 98% — one bad year from forced repayment. At 15% and above it lapses. The strategy isn't wrong in kind; it's wrong by roughly a factor of ten in degree.
