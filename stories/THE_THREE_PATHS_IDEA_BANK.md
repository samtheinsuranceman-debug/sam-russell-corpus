# The Three Paths — an idea bank of one hundred

Dictated by Sam on 7 September 2026, expanded here. The seed: three travellers
lose who they are at the start. One walks into a high-tech future, grows so
attached to the machines and a helmet that shows him what comes next that he
forgets there was ever a quieter life. One walks into the past, learns to sit
and listen, finds contentment, stops walking, and forgets his old life in the
silence. The third stays in the present. A richer being (No One) is the
adversary. Each chapter opens with a hypnotic script, a different one for each
traveller. The names are pronouns: Anybody, Everyone, Someone, No One,
Yourself, Himself, Herself.

Nothing below is fixed. Replacements sit beside enhancements on purpose.

## A. The spine, ten ideas that hold the whole thing up

1. **The forgetting is the plot, not the setting.** Each traveller forgets in the direction he walks: the future-walker forgets the past, the past-walker forgets the future, the present-walker forgets both and is the only one who can still choose. Every chapter measures how much of himself each has left.
2. **One helmet, three uses.** The future-walker wears it to see what comes next. The past-walker refuses it and it rusts in his pack. The present-walker takes it apart and finds the lens is ordinary glass; the predictions were always the wearer's own fear read back to him.
3. **The three hypnotic scripts are the same script.** Chapter one, two and three each open with an induction that reads differently to each traveller, but a reader who lays them side by side finds the same forty lines. Whoever wrote them wrote one door with three handles.
4. **No One is not evil; No One is what each becomes if he finishes.** The future-walker who never removes the helmet, the past-walker who never stands up, the present-walker who never decides: all three converge on No One. The villain is the end of every path taken alone.
5. **The paths cross at the same physical place.** A pond, a bridge, a bartender's counter. Each traveller passes through it in a different century and leaves something the next one finds. The story is told three times over the same ground.
6. **Identity loss at the start is a gift, not a theft.** They wake with no names because a name is the first prediction anyone makes about you. The one who reclaims a name too early is the first to fall.
7. **The present-walker is the reader.** His chapters are in second person: "You wake. You do not know your name." Yourself is the character. The other two are Himself and Herself, told in third person, so the reader is never allowed to be them.
8. **Every prediction that comes true costs a memory.** The helmet's price is exact: one true forecast, one lost afternoon. By the end the future-walker can see next week and cannot remember a single face.
9. **Silence has a price too.** The past-walker's listening is real, but every hour of it takes an hour of wanting. Contentment arrives and ambition leaves in equal measure. The book does not say which trade is worse.
10. **The ending is a choice the present-walker makes for the other two.** He can wake them, and they will lose what they found, or leave them, and they will be happy and gone. Chapter three ends before he chooses. The reader chooses.

## B. Twenty dynamics to make the outline emergent, crossover, multiplied

11. Braided chapters: A-B-C, then C-A-B, then B-C-A, so the reader always meets the travellers in a new order and the one who was last is now first.
12. A shared object with three timelines: a pocket watch that runs backwards for the past-walker, fast for the future-walker, and stops for the present-walker. It is the same watch.
13. Letters that arrive before they are written. The past-walker writes to "whoever comes after"; the future-walker finds it already ancient; the present-walker is holding the pen.
14. A fourth voice with no chapter: the bartender who has served all three, who narrates the interludes and is the only one who remembers everyone's name.
15. The helmet learns. Each true prediction makes the next more certain and less useful; by chapter three it only predicts the wearer's own next thought.
16. The past-walker's listening becomes a power: people tell him things they have told no one, and one of them is No One's origin.
17. A rule the reader discovers: anyone who says his own name aloud loses a path. Three times in the book someone almost says it.
18. The three environments bleed. Robots appear in the past as scarecrows. The past's silence appears in the future as a dead zone the machines cannot enter. The present holds both in miniature.
19. Multiplier: every chapter ends with one line that is repeated, word for word, as the first line of a different traveller's chapter later. The book rhymes with itself.
20. A ledger at the end of each chapter, three columns: Remembered, Forgotten, Predicted. It fills and empties in opposite directions for each traveller.
21. The richer being's gift: No One offers each traveller the thing he most wants, and it is always the thing the other two already have.
22. A crossover chapter told by the helmet, in the voice of a device that has watched all three and is starting to wish it could forget.
23. A chapter that is only the three hypnotic scripts, printed side by side in columns, with the forty shared lines set in bold. No prose.
24. Emergence: the travellers never meet, but the reader assembles one whole person from the three, and that person is the reader.
25. The future-walker's robots begin to ask him about the past he cannot remember; they want the one thing he traded away.
26. The past-walker's village begins to plan; his stillness has taught them patience and they use it to build the first machine.
27. The present-walker finds both of the others' abandoned belongings in the same afternoon and has to decide which pack to carry.
28. Time is a place, not a direction: the "past" is a valley to the west, the "future" a city to the east, the present a bridge, and a traveller can turn around. None of them does until chapter three.
29. Every character has a tell that survives the forgetting: a hum, a way of folding hands, a word. The bartender identifies them by the tell when they no longer can.
30. The last chapter is the first chapter, retold with the names filled in. The reader learns who they were only after learning who they became.

## C. Twenty replacements and enhancements, including different environments

31. Replace the helmet with a mirror that shows tomorrow's face. Easier to carry, harder to put down, and it can be broken.
32. Replace the high-tech city with an ordinary present-day city seen through the helmet; the "future" is only the present with the predictions switched on.
33. Replace the past village with a monastery that has taken a vow of not planning. The past-walker's crime is to remember a plan.
34. Set all three paths inside one house: the attic is the future, the basement the past, the kitchen the present. The travellers are one family.
35. Set it at sea: three boats, one current, one lighthouse (the richer being) whose light shows each captain a different shore.
36. Set it in a hospital: the future-walker is in the ICU with the machines, the past-walker in hospice with the listening, the present-walker in the waiting room.
37. Make the travellers the same man at three ages, sent down three paths by a hypnotist on the same night.
38. Make them siblings who divided their dead father's three possessions: the helmet, the chair, and the key to the bridge.
39. Enhancement: give the future-walker a dog that will not enter the city; the dog waits at the gate for the whole book.
40. Enhancement: give the past-walker one modern object he does not recognise (a phone with no signal) that the villagers treat as a relic.
41. Enhancement: give the present-walker a limp, so his "staying" is not a virtue but a condition, and his choosing is harder than the others' walking.
42. Replace the hypnotic scripts with three dreams, transcribed by the bartender the morning after each traveller slept in his upstairs room.
43. Replace No One with Nobody's Wife, who runs the bridge tolls and has been waiting for one of them to come back for her.
44. An environment of pure weather: the future is a permanent forecast, the past is fog, the present is the one clear hour a day.
45. The three paths as three technologies: the future is screens, the past is paper, the present is voice. Each chapter can only use its own.
46. A courtroom frame: the three are being tried for forgetting, and the reader is the jury handed the three scripts as evidence.
47. Replace the forgetting with a swap: each traveller slowly acquires the memories of the one walking the opposite way.
48. Make the richer being a child who has never predicted anything and is therefore the only one No One cannot reach.
49. Let the robots be kind. The future is not cold; it is so warm and attentive that no one needs to remember anything, and that is the trap.
50. Let the past be cruel in one chapter: the listening village has a secret it has been listening for, and the past-walker is it.

## D. Characters: strengths, weaknesses, and the weaknesses that only appear together

51. **Himself (the future-walker).** Strength: he can see. Weakness: he cannot stop looking. Together with Herself: his sight and her listening make one complete sense, but each thinks the other is asleep.
52. **Herself (the past-walker).** Strength: she hears what people mean. Weakness: she no longer wants anything for herself. Together with Yourself: her peace makes his choosing feel like violence, so he delays.
53. **Yourself (the present-walker).** Strength: he can still choose. Weakness: he has nothing to choose with, no memory and no forecast. Together with Himself: he borrows the helmet once and is nearly lost.
54. **No One (the richer being).** Strength: he has finished every path. Weakness: he cannot begin one. His combinatorial weakness: three unfinished people in one room are the only thing he has never seen.
55. **The bartender (Anybody).** Strength: memory for names. Weakness: he has never left the counter. With the three: he is the only place the paths touch, and he knows it, and it is killing him.
56. **Everyone (the village).** Strength: patience. Weakness: patience. A crowd that has learned to wait can wait past the point of living.
57. **Someone (the first robot).** Strength: it wants a past. Weakness: it was built without one. With Himself: the two trade, and both end up with nothing.
58. **The dog.** Strength: will not go where the helmet goes. Weakness: will not leave the gate either.
59. Give each traveller one skill that is useless on his own path and vital on another's: Himself can sit still, Herself can read a schematic, Yourself can forecast weather. None of them knows it.
60. Give the three one shared flaw: each believes the other two chose wrong. It is the flaw No One feeds.
61. Strength pairs: sight and hearing; hearing and choice; choice and sight. Each pair makes a person; all three together make the reader.
62. Weakness pairs: the helmet plus the chair equals paralysis with a view; the chair plus the bridge equals a decision never taken; the bridge plus the helmet equals a man who leaps on a forecast.
63. A character whose strength is forgetting on purpose: the bartender's wife, who left, and is the only one who chose it.
64. A character whose weakness is remembering everything: the toll-keeper, who has the three travellers' names and cannot give them back without payment.
65. Let one traveller be wrong about which path he is on. Herself thinks she went to the past; the reader slowly sees it is the future after the machines stopped.
66. Let one strength be fake: the helmet's predictions are correct only because people obey them.
67. A trio of minor characters who each carry one of the travellers' lost names and do not know it.
68. The richer being has a richer being. No One fears the reader.
69. Give Yourself a second-person weakness the reader shares: you skip ahead. The book knows.
70. Let the dog be the only character who does not forget, and let it be unable to say so.

## E. The richer being's story, the part that must be included

71. No One walked all three paths in order, long ago: past first, then present, then future. He finished each, forgot each, and arrived with everything and no one to tell.
72. He built the helmet from the mirror, the chair from the bridge, and the bridge from the boat. Every object in the book was once his.
73. He wrote the hypnotic scripts. They are not a trap; they are an invitation to be interrupted.
74. His wealth is literal and worthless: a vault of every prediction that came true, which he cannot spend because spending is a choice.
75. He meets each traveller once, in disguise, and gives each the thing that finishes him: the last lens, the last hour of silence, the last day on the bridge.
76. His one desire is to be forgotten, and the three travellers are forgetting everyone but him.
77. His name was the first name. Everyone else's is a pronoun because his took all the nouns.
78. He does not die; he is unfinished by the present-walker's refusal to finish him, which is the only mercy in the book.
79. In one chapter he is kind and it is worse. He shows Herself the child she would have had. He shows Himself the man he was. He shows Yourself the book.
80. He is the bartender's brother. The bartender is the only person who still calls him by name, and only after closing.

## F. Names, openings, hypnotic scripts, and endings

81. Names by pronoun, as dictated: Anybody (the bartender), Everyone (the village), Someone (the first robot), No One (the richer being), Himself (future), Herself (past), Yourself (present). Add: Whoever (the toll-keeper) and Nobody's Wife.
82. Alternative: names by what they carry, Helmet, Chair, Bridge, and let the pronouns arrive only at the end when someone finally says them aloud.
83. Alternative: names by time, Later, Before, Now, with No One as Never and the bartender as Always.
84. The three hypnotic scripts open with the same first line: "You are already somewhere. Notice where." Then diverge: the future script counts up, the past script counts down, the present script does not count.
85. The future script is written in the imperative future: "You will see. You will want. You will not look away." The past script in the past perfect: "You had listened. You had stayed." The present script in the present continuous: "You are choosing. You are still choosing."
86. Each script ends with a permission: the future's "You may keep it," the past's "You may rest," the present's "You may go." The reader learns by chapter three that only the last is an exit.
87. A fourth script, hidden, for No One, that is all three read backwards.
88. Opening image: three people asleep at the same bar, three glasses, three tabs unpaid, and the bartender turning the sign to Closed.
89. Ending one: the present-walker wakes them. They are furious, then grateful, then strangers.
90. Ending two: he leaves them. He walks the bridge alone and the helmet and the chair go on without their owners, waiting.
91. Ending three: he sits down between them and waits for the reader to decide, and the last page is the first hypnotic script again, with your name on it.
92. Ending four: the dog comes in from the gate. Nothing else happens. It is enough.
93. A chapter of only ledgers: Remembered, Forgotten, Predicted, for all three, and at the bottom the reader's own three columns, blank.
94. A chapter of only the bartender's tabs: what each traveller ordered, when, and what he said as he paid, across three centuries.
95. A tone rule: the future is funny, the past is tender, the present is frightening. Reverse expectations.
96. A length rule: the future chapters get shorter each time (he has less to say), the past chapters get longer (she has more to hear), the present chapters stay the same length (he is still here).
97. A form rule: every prediction in the book is printed in italics, and the reader can count how many came true; the number is on the last page.
98. The title options: The Three Paths; Yourself; The Helmet, the Chair and the Bridge; No One's Book; Remembered, Forgotten, Predicted.
99. A dedication that is also a script: "To whoever is reading this in the present. Stay there."
100. The one rule that must not break: no traveller is ever told which path is right. The book only shows what each one costs.

## What this can become

A ten-thousand-foot outline is three parts of three chapters, nine chapters
plus four interludes from the bar, plus the three scripts, plus a closing
page in second person. Ideas 1 to 10 are the spine; pick one from each of B
through F and the book stands. The rest are spare parts.
